## Start with the idea

A multiple-choice question is not only a test of memory. It asks you to recognise what concept is being tested, connect it to the options and choose the most defensible answer.

If you only memorise answer keys, a small change in wording can make a familiar question feel completely new. A better approach is to learn the reason behind the correct option.

## 1. Read the question before looking at the options

First identify what the question is asking:

- A definition?
- A mechanism of action?
- A drug–adverse effect association?
- An instrument or apparatus?
- A calculation?
- A legal schedule or regulatory fact?
- A comparison between two related concepts?

Underline the key phrase mentally. For example, “inhibits dihydropteroate synthase” points towards the bacterial folate pathway, while “blocks AT1 receptors” points towards angiotensin-receptor blockers.

## 2. Predict the answer in your own words

Before scanning the options, try to recall the concept.

Example:

**Question:** Which class blocks the AT1 receptor?

Your concept recall should be: “angiotensin receptor blocker”.

Now compare that idea with the options. This reduces the chance of being distracted by familiar but incorrect drug names.

## 3. Eliminate options for a reason

Do not eliminate an option merely because it looks unfamiliar. Ask why it does not fit.

For example, if the question asks for a tablet lubricant:

- A binder helps particles hold together.
- A disintegrant helps a tablet break apart.
- A diluent adds bulk.
- A lubricant reduces friction during manufacture.

This comparison is more useful than memorising a single one-line association.

## 4. Show your working in calculation questions

For numerical questions:

1. Write the formula.
2. Identify the given values and units.
3. Convert units before substitution if needed.
4. Substitute carefully.
5. Check whether the answer is physically and mathematically reasonable.

For example, Beer–Lambert law is:

\[
A = \varepsilon b c
\]

If molar absorptivity and path length remain constant, absorbance is directly proportional to concentration. The question may test the relationship rather than a difficult calculation.

## 5. Review the distractors, not only the answer

After attempting a question, write one short note:

- Why is the correct option correct?
- Why was my chosen option wrong?
- Which concept did I confuse?
- What clue in the question should I notice next time?

A useful error log can have four columns:

| Topic | My mistake | Correct concept | Next revision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tablet excipients | Mixed up binder and lubricant | Their functions are different | Revise excipient table |
| RAAS | Confused ACE inhibitor and ARB | One inhibits ACE; the other blocks AT1 | Draw the pathway |
| UV spectroscopy | Forgot log relationship | A = −log T | Solve 3 examples |

## 6. Use spaced revision instead of repeating everything in one sitting

A practical study cycle:

- **First attempt:** solve the question and understand the explanation.
- **Next day:** revisit questions you got wrong.
- **Later in the week:** solve a mixed set without looking at notes.
- **Before the exam:** review the error log and high-yield comparisons.

The exact schedule can be adjusted to your timetable. The important idea is to revisit material after some forgetting has occurred, rather than only rereading it immediately.

## 7. Keep PYQs and practice questions separate

This matters when tracking your preparation.

- **PYQ:** a question whose exam, year/session and source can be verified.
- **Practice question:** an original question written to test a concept.
- **Mock test:** a timed set designed to simulate an exam session.

A generated question should never be labelled as an official previous-year question. A PYQ should retain enough source information to trace where it came from.

## 8. A repeatable 5-step MCQ method

1. Identify the subject and topic.
2. Find the exact concept being tested.
3. Predict the answer before reading options.
4. Eliminate distractors with a reason.
5. Review the explanation and record the mistake.

<Callout type="tip" title="A useful rule">
Do not count a question as “learned” just because you selected the correct option. Count it as learned when you can explain why the correct answer is right and why the closest distractor is wrong.
</Callout>
