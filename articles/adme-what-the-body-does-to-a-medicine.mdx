## Start with the idea

A medicine does not simply enter the body and start working. After it is given, the body moves it, changes some of it and eventually removes it. These steps help explain why two medicines can have different onset times, durations and dosing schedules.

Pharmacokinetics is often summarised as **ADME**:

- **A — Absorption:** how a drug reaches the bloodstream from its administration site.
- **D — Distribution:** how it moves between blood and tissues.
- **M — Metabolism:** how the body chemically changes it.
- **E — Excretion:** how the drug and its metabolites leave the body.

A simple memory line: **ADME = what the body does to the drug.** Pharmacodynamics is the other side of the story: what the drug does to the body.

## 1. Absorption: getting into the bloodstream

Imagine swallowing a tablet. Before the drug can be absorbed, the dosage form usually has to release the drug. The drug then needs to dissolve and cross biological membranes to reach systemic circulation.

Absorption depends on several things:

- The route: oral, sublingual, intramuscular, inhaled, transdermal and others.
- The dosage form and how quickly it releases the drug.
- Solubility, ionisation and membrane permeability.
- Blood flow at the absorption site.
- Food, gastrointestinal conditions and interactions with other substances.

**Important exception:** an intravenous dose is placed directly into systemic circulation. It does not need an absorption step, and its absolute bioavailability is 100%.

### First-pass metabolism

After absorption from the gut, many orally administered drugs travel through the portal circulation to the liver before reaching the rest of the body. Enzymes in the gut wall and liver may metabolise part of the dose during this first journey. This is called the **first-pass effect**.

That is one reason why the same drug can have different bioavailability by different routes.

## 2. Distribution: where the drug travels

Once in the bloodstream, a drug can move into tissues and body fluids. This movement is called distribution.

Think of the bloodstream as a transport network. A drug may remain mostly in the blood, enter a particular organ, or spread widely into tissues.

Distribution is influenced by:

- Blood flow to the tissue.
- Lipid solubility and ionisation.
- Molecular size.
- Binding to plasma proteins such as albumin.
- Barriers such as the blood–brain barrier.

### Free drug and protein-bound drug

Some drugs bind reversibly to proteins in plasma. The drug that is not bound is called the **free** or **unbound** fraction.

For many drugs, the free fraction is the part available to cross membranes, interact with receptors and undergo elimination. Protein binding is reversible, so bound drug can act as a reservoir as free drug leaves the circulation.

### Volume of distribution (Vd)

Vd is an *apparent* volume that relates the amount of drug in the body to its measured plasma concentration:

\[
V_d = \frac{\text{Amount of drug in the body}}{\text{Plasma drug concentration}}
\]

A low Vd generally suggests that more drug remains in the plasma. A high Vd suggests more extensive movement into tissues. It is not a literal measurement of the body's fluid volume.

## 3. Metabolism: the body changes the drug

Metabolism, also called biotransformation, is the chemical modification of a drug by enzymes.

The liver is a major site, although metabolism can also occur in the gut wall, kidneys, blood and other tissues.

Two broad groups of reactions are commonly taught:

| Reaction group | What happens | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Phase I | Oxidation, reduction or hydrolysis may introduce or expose a functional group | Many reactions involve cytochrome P450 enzymes |
| Phase II | Conjugation attaches a group to the drug or its metabolite | Glucuronidation, sulfation and acetylation |

Do not assume that metabolism always inactivates a drug. It may:

- Inactivate the parent drug.
- Produce an active metabolite.
- Activate a prodrug.
- Occasionally produce a metabolite responsible for toxicity.

## 4. Excretion: leaving the body

Excretion is the removal of a drug or its metabolites from the body. The kidneys are a major route, but drugs can also leave through bile and faeces, lungs, sweat, saliva and other routes.

For renal excretion, remember three processes:

1. **Glomerular filtration:** unbound drug can pass from plasma into the filtrate.
2. **Tubular secretion:** transporters move certain drugs from blood into the tubule.
3. **Tubular reabsorption:** some drug can move back from tubular fluid into blood.

The extent of renal elimination can be affected by kidney function, urine pH, protein binding and drug properties.

## 5. The terms that connect ADME to dosing

| Term | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| Bioavailability (F) | Fraction of a dose reaching systemic circulation unchanged |
| Cmax | Highest measured plasma concentration after a dose |
| Tmax | Time taken to reach Cmax |
| AUC | Overall drug exposure across a concentration–time curve |
| Half-life (t½) | Time for plasma concentration or amount in the body to fall by half during the relevant elimination phase |
| Clearance (CL) | Volume of plasma from which drug is removed per unit time |

These terms are connected, but they do not mean the same thing. For example, AUC is commonly used to describe the extent of systemic exposure, while Cmax and Tmax help describe peak concentration and the time to reach it.

## 6. Put the whole journey together

A tablet may release its drug in the gastrointestinal tract. The drug dissolves and some of it crosses the intestinal wall. It enters portal blood, where some may be metabolised before reaching systemic circulation. The drug that reaches the circulation distributes into tissues, may undergo further metabolism and is eventually eliminated.

That journey is why formulation, route, liver function, kidney function and drug interactions can all influence the concentration–time profile.

<Callout type="exam" title="GPAT memory check">
- ADME describes the body's handling of a drug.
- Pharmacodynamics describes the drug's effects on the body.
- IV administration bypasses absorption.
- AUC is associated with total systemic exposure.
- Vd is an apparent volume, not a literal body compartment.
</Callout>
