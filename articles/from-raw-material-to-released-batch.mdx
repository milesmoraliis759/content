## Start with the idea

A medicine is not considered good quality just because the finished tablet looks correct or one laboratory test passes. Quality has to be planned into the process, protected during manufacturing and supported by reliable records.

A useful way to understand pharmaceutical manufacturing is to follow one imaginary batch from the arrival of its raw materials to the decision that it can be released.

This is a learning overview, not a site operating procedure. Actual steps and responsibilities depend on the product, applicable regulations and approved procedures.

## 1. Materials arrive — identity and status matter

Before a material is used, the organisation needs controls for receipt, identification, sampling, testing, approval and storage.

A container should not be treated as approved merely because it has arrived at the factory. Its identity and quality status must be established through the approved material-control system.

Typical questions include:

- Is the material correctly identified?
- Is the supplier approved for this material?
- Is the container intact and labelled correctly?
- Has the required sampling and testing been completed?
- Is the material released, quarantined or rejected?

**Quarantine** means the material is held under controlled status until an authorised decision is made. The exact status labels and electronic controls are site-specific.

## 2. Dispensing and manufacturing — follow the approved instructions

Once materials are released, the manufacturing process follows approved instructions such as the master formula and batch manufacturing record.

For a typical wet-granulated tablet, the broad sequence may include:

1. Dispensing and sifting.
2. Dry mixing.
3. Binder preparation and addition.
4. Wet granulation.
5. Drying.
6. Sizing and milling.
7. Final blending and lubrication.
8. Compression.
9. Coating, if required.
10. Packaging and labelling.

Not every tablet uses wet granulation. Direct compression and dry granulation are also used, depending on formulation and process design.

At each stage, trained personnel follow the approved procedure and record what they actually did. A record is not paperwork added after the work; it is evidence of how the batch was made.

## 3. In-process checks — detect variation while work is happening

In-process controls help check whether the process is behaving as expected before the batch reaches the finished-product stage.

For tablet manufacture, examples may include:

- Tablet appearance.
- Average weight and weight variation.
- Thickness and hardness.
- Friability, where required by the process or specification.
- Disintegration or other relevant performance checks.
- Compression-machine settings and process observations.

The tests, frequency, sample size and acceptance criteria must come from the approved product and process documents. There is no single universal set of limits for every tablet.

## 4. Quality Control — test samples against specifications

Quality Control (QC) performs or oversees defined sampling and testing activities. Depending on the product, this may include:

- Identification of the active ingredient.
- Assay of drug content.
- Related-substances or impurity testing.
- Dissolution testing.
- Microbiological testing, where applicable.
- Testing of packaging materials and starting materials.

A laboratory result answers a specific question about the sample and the method used. It does not, by itself, prove that every manufacturing step was controlled correctly.

## 5. Quality Assurance — review the system and the batch story

Quality Assurance (QA) has a broader quality-system role. It helps ensure that the organisation has suitable procedures and that quality-related activities are controlled and documented.

Examples can include:

- Review of batch manufacturing and packaging records.
- Oversight of deviations and investigations.
- Change control.
- CAPA (corrective and preventive action).
- Training and document control.
- Supplier and audit programmes.
- Review of relevant laboratory and production records.

QA and QC work together, but their responsibilities are not identical. The exact organisational structure varies between manufacturers.

## 6. What happens when something goes wrong?

Suppose a process observation falls outside an approved range, a label reconciliation does not match, or a laboratory result is unexpected.

The correct response is not to hide the event or silently change a record. It is to follow the site's approved reporting and investigation process.

A deviation is assessed, its potential impact is considered, and an investigation may identify a root cause. CAPA may then be used to address the cause and reduce the chance of recurrence.

A correction fixes an observed issue; a preventive action aims to stop a similar issue from happening again. The investigation and action must be evidence-based.

## 7. Batch disposition — a decision supported by evidence

Before a batch is released, authorised quality personnel review the required records and results according to the applicable quality system and regulations.

The decision is based on the whole batch history, not just the final assay result:

- Were the approved materials used?
- Were manufacturing steps completed and recorded?
- Were required in-process controls satisfactory?
- Were laboratory results reviewed?
- Were deviations and investigations appropriately assessed?
- Were packaging and labelling controls completed?
- Is the batch documentation complete and acceptable?

A passing test result cannot compensate for an uncontrolled process or unreliable records.

<Callout type="note" title="The central idea">
GMP is about preventing mix-ups, contamination, errors and unreliable records through controlled people, premises, equipment, materials, procedures and documentation. Quality is built throughout the process — not tested into the medicine at the end.
</Callout>

## A simple comparison: QA, QC and Production

| Function | Main contribution | Example question |
|---|---|---|
| Production | Performs manufacturing according to approved instructions | Was this process step performed and recorded correctly? |
| QC | Samples and tests against specifications | Does this sample meet the test requirement? |
| QA | Oversees the quality system and batch review | Is there enough reliable evidence to support the quality decision? |

These are simplified learning descriptions. Actual job responsibilities are defined by the organisation's quality system and applicable rules.

## GPAT / industrial learning takeaways

- GMP aims to reduce risks such as contamination, mix-ups and incorrect labelling.
- Production follows approved manufacturing instructions.
- QC focuses on sampling, testing and results against specifications.
- QA supports the broader quality system and batch-record review.
- Deviations should be reported and investigated through approved procedures.
- Batch release is an authorised quality decision based on the complete evidence.
