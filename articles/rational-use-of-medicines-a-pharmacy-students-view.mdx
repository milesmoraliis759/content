## Start with the idea

A medicine can be scientifically effective and still be used incorrectly. The wrong medicine, dose, duration or route can reduce benefit and create avoidable harm.

**Rational Use of Medicines (RUM)** means that people receive medicines appropriate to their clinical needs, in doses that meet their individual requirements, for an adequate period and at a cost they and their community can afford.

This definition comes from the World Health Organization (WHO). It is a useful starting point for every pharmacy student because it connects pharmacology with real-world medicine use.

## 1. What does irrational use look like?

Examples include:

- Using an antibiotic for an illness that does not need an antibiotic.
- Taking a medicine at an incorrect dose or for an unsuitable duration.
- Using several medicines without reviewing possible duplication or interactions.
- Choosing an injection when a suitable oral formulation would meet the need.
- Self-medicating with prescription-only medicines without appropriate assessment.
- Not following the agreed dosing schedule because instructions were unclear or access was difficult.

The issue is not simply that a patient has made a mistake. Prescribing, dispensing, communication, affordability, health literacy and medicine availability can all influence how medicines are used.

## 2. Why does it matter?

Inappropriate medicine use can lead to:

- Treatment failure.
- Avoidable adverse effects.
- Antimicrobial resistance when antimicrobials are misused.
- Unnecessary costs for patients and health systems.
- Wasted medicines and healthcare resources.

WHO highlights inappropriate prescribing, dispensing and use as a major global problem. The exact scale varies by setting and the data should be read in context; the central lesson is that medicine use needs active improvement.

## 3. Where can a pharmacist make a difference?

A pharmacist can contribute at several points:

### Before dispensing

- Check that the prescription is complete and understandable.
- Identify possible dose, duplication or interaction concerns within the pharmacist's professional scope.
- Refer unclear or potentially unsafe prescriptions to the prescriber.
- Check legal and storage requirements.

### During dispensing

- Select the correct medicine, strength and dosage form.
- Check the label and quantity.
- Explain how and when to take the medicine.
- Explain relevant storage instructions and important precautions.
- Use language the patient can understand.

### After dispensing

- Encourage patients to ask questions.
- Help identify practical barriers to adherence.
- Reinforce antimicrobial stewardship messages.
- Refer suspected adverse reactions or treatment concerns through the appropriate channels.

A pharmacist does not replace the prescriber. Good practice involves communication and working within professional responsibilities.

## 4. A simple example

Imagine a patient receives an oral antibiotic prescription but is unsure whether to stop when symptoms improve.

A useful counselling conversation should not invent a new treatment plan. It should:

1. Confirm the instructions on the prescription and dispensed label.
2. Explain the prescribed schedule in clear language.
3. Encourage the patient to follow the prescriber's instructions.
4. Explain whom to contact if a dose is missed, an adverse effect occurs or the patient is unsure.
5. Avoid recommending leftover antibiotics or sharing medicines.

The key skill is clear, accurate communication — not giving unsupported advice.

## 5. Rational use and antimicrobial stewardship

Antimicrobial stewardship aims to support appropriate antimicrobial selection, dose, route and duration, while considering patient outcomes and resistance.

For a pharmacy student, remember:

- Antibiotics treat bacterial infections, not viral infections.
- The need for an antimicrobial depends on clinical assessment.
- The correct agent, dose, route and duration matter.
- Unnecessary or inappropriate use contributes to antimicrobial resistance.
- Patients should not self-start, share or reuse prescription antibiotics.

## 6. A quick checklist for pharmacy students

When reviewing a medicine-use situation, ask:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Is the medicine appropriate for the condition? | Avoids unnecessary or unsuitable therapy |
| Is the dose suitable for the person? | Age, organ function and other factors may matter |
| Is the route appropriate? | Route affects absorption, convenience and safety |
| Is the duration clear? | Too short or too long can cause problems |
| Are there interactions or duplications? | Helps identify preventable harm |
| Does the patient understand the instructions? | Communication affects correct use |
| Is the medicine accessible and affordable? | Practical barriers can disrupt treatment |

This is a learning checklist, not a substitute for clinical assessment or a formal medication review.

<Callout type="exam" title="Remember for exams">
Rational use is not simply “use fewer medicines”. It means using the **right medicine, for the right patient, at the right dose, by the right route, for the right duration and at an affordable cost**, guided by evidence and clinical judgement.
</Callout>
