## A simple question that made me look twice at toothpaste

Did you brush your teeth today? If yes, do you know which toothpaste you used—and why you chose it?

Most of us pick a toothpaste because we recognise the brand, like the flavour, see a whitening claim, or have used it for years. I started looking more closely at toothpaste labels and made a handwritten comparison chart of products and ingredients. That exercise raised an important question: **are we judging toothpaste by its brand and marketing, or by what its ingredients are actually meant to do?**

My first chart grouped products into regular, herbal and “best” options. But checking the scientific literature taught me that a simple good-versus-bad ranking is not reliable. An ingredient name alone does not tell us whether a product is harmful. Its concentration, purpose, how it is used, the person using it and the quality of the evidence all matter.

This is a student-led, evidence-informed explainer—not a systematic review, laboratory test or endorsement of any toothpaste brand.

## First, what is toothpaste designed to do?

Toothpaste (also called dentifrice) is used with a toothbrush to clean teeth and deliver ingredients that support oral health. Depending on the formulation, it may help:

- remove plaque and food debris through brushing;
- prevent tooth decay (dental caries);
- reduce tooth sensitivity;
- help control gingivitis or tartar;
- improve breath or remove surface stains.

Different ingredients have different jobs. Some are active ingredients; others help the paste foam, taste pleasant, remain stable or keep the formulation usable.

## Ingredients worth understanding

### 1. Fluoride: the ingredient I would not dismiss

Fluoride is included in many toothpastes because it helps prevent dental caries. It supports remineralisation—the repair of early mineral loss in enamel—and makes the tooth surface more resistant to acid attacks.

The evidence for fluoride toothpaste in preventing tooth decay is strong. A Cochrane review found high-certainty evidence that toothpaste containing 1,000–1,250 parts per million (ppm) fluoride reduces tooth decay compared with non-fluoride toothpaste in the populations studied.

**Does that mean fluoride is poisonous?** Not when used appropriately in toothpaste. The risk depends on the amount swallowed and the exposure. Toothpaste is meant to be spat out, not eaten. Repeated excessive ingestion during tooth development can contribute to dental fluorosis, while a large accidental ingestion can cause acute toxicity and needs prompt professional assessment.

The statement in my original handwritten chart that “about 2.8 grams of fluoride can kill a 50 kg adult” is not a safe or appropriate way to explain toothpaste risk. I have not retained it as a clinical threshold: toxicity depends on the actual fluoride dose, chemical form, body weight and circumstances. Do not use a number from an informal chart to assess a possible poisoning.

**Practical point:** choose a fluoride toothpaste appropriate for your age and dental needs, use the amount advised on the label or by your dental professional, and spit it out after brushing. For children, supervise brushing and follow age-appropriate quantity guidance.

### 2. Sodium lauryl sulphate (SLS): a foaming agent

Sodium lauryl sulphate (SLS) is a surfactant. It helps toothpaste spread and produce foam, which many people associate with a “clean” feeling. Foam itself is not proof that a toothpaste cleans better.

Some people find SLS-containing toothpaste irritating. Research has explored whether SLS-free toothpaste may help people who experience recurrent aphthous ulcers (mouth ulcers). A clinical trial and a later scoping review reported possible benefits for some outcomes, such as ulcer duration or pain, but the evidence is limited and does not establish that SLS causes ulcers in everyone.

If you repeatedly experience irritation or mouth ulcers, discuss it with a dentist. They may suggest trying an SLS-free toothpaste while checking for other possible causes.

### 3. Triclosan: distinguish a safety question from a proven harm

Triclosan is an antibacterial ingredient that has been used in some personal-care products, including certain toothpaste formulations. Its safety has been examined because of questions about possible endocrine-related effects.

That does not justify saying that ordinary use of every toothpaste containing triclosan causes reproductive damage, liver cancer or endocrine disease. The European Commission’s Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety assessed triclosan in cosmetic products and concluded that its use as a preservative in toothpaste at 0.3%, when used individually, was safe under the conditions it assessed. This is a product- and concentration-specific assessment, not a blanket statement about every formulation or every possible exposure.

The useful lesson is to avoid dramatic claims that go beyond the evidence. Ingredient status and permitted uses can also differ by jurisdiction and change over time.

### 4. Preservatives, colours and flavouring agents

Preservatives help protect a formulation from microbial growth and maintain product quality during storage. Colours and flavouring agents mainly affect appearance and taste.

Some individuals can be sensitive or allergic to particular ingredients, but it is inaccurate to label all preservatives or artificial colours as harmful to everyone. If a product consistently causes burning, rash around the mouth or another reaction, stop using it and seek advice from a dentist or healthcare professional.

## Herbal, natural and “organic” toothpaste: is it automatically better?

Not necessarily. “Natural”, “herbal” and “organic” describe marketing or sourcing claims; they do not, by themselves, prove that a toothpaste is safer or more effective.

A herbal formulation may contain plant-derived ingredients, but it still needs to be evaluated for quality, safety and evidence of benefit. Most importantly, check whether it contains an effective anticaries ingredient such as fluoride if cavity prevention is your goal. A product being plant-based does not automatically mean it provides the same level of cavity protection as a fluoride toothpaste.

Certification can help verify a particular standard or production claim, but a certification logo should not be mistaken for proof of superior cavity prevention. Read what the certification actually covers.

## Why a brand-by-brand ranking can mislead

My handwritten chart listed several familiar toothpaste brands and grouped them by type. I would not treat that chart as a definitive safety ranking.

A brand may sell multiple variants, and ingredient lists can differ between variants, countries and reformulations. A brand name alone cannot tell us whether a particular tube contains fluoride, SLS, triclosan or a specific preservative. Always check the ingredient list and the active ingredient information on the exact product you are buying.

| What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Fluoride compound and concentration | Helps you understand the product’s cavity-prevention role |
| Desensitising ingredients | May be useful if you have dentine sensitivity |
| SLS | Worth checking if you have recurring irritation or ulcers |
| Allergens or flavouring agents | Important if you have a known sensitivity |
| Directions and age guidance | Helps prevent inappropriate use or swallowing |
| Claims and certification | Check what the claim or seal actually verifies |

## A practical way to choose toothpaste

1. **Start with your oral-health need.** Cavity prevention, sensitivity, gum concerns and whitening are different goals.
2. **Check the active ingredients.** Do not choose only by colour, flavour, “herbal” wording or brand popularity.
3. **Do not assume foam means effectiveness.** SLS is mainly a foaming/cleansing surfactant.
4. **Be cautious with absolute claims.** “Chemical-free”, “100% safe” and “toxic” are not meaningful conclusions without evidence and context.
5. **Ask a dentist if you have recurring problems.** Persistent ulcers, sensitivity, bleeding gums or pain deserve proper assessment.
6. **Brush consistently.** Technique, frequency and regular dental care matter just as much as the tube you select.

## What I learned from this comparison

The biggest lesson was not that branded toothpaste is bad or that organic toothpaste is always good. It was that **we should understand a product before judging it**.

Fluoride has a well-established role in preventing tooth decay. SLS may be irritating for some people, especially those who experience recurrent ulcers, but it is not automatically harmful to everyone. Triclosan and other ingredients need to be discussed using concentration-specific safety assessments rather than alarming headlines. Herbal claims also need evidence.

So, before switching toothpaste, read the label, identify your oral-health needs and use reliable dental guidance. The best choice is not necessarily the most expensive, most foamy or most “natural” one—it is the formulation that is appropriate for your needs and supported by evidence.
