## Start with the idea

After you take a medicine, some of the dose may reach the bloodstream and some may not. Bioavailability tells us how much gets into circulation and how quickly.

<Definition term="Bioavailability (BA)">The rate and extent to which the active moiety is absorbed from a drug product and becomes available at the site of action, or in systemic circulation when assessed using systemic exposure.</Definition>

For most conventional systemic medicines, bioavailability is evaluated using plasma concentration–time data.

## 1. Important pharmacokinetic measures

| Parameter | Meaning | What it helps describe |
|---|---|---|
| AUC | Area under the plasma concentration–time curve | Extent of systemic exposure |
| Cmax | Maximum observed plasma concentration | Peak exposure |
| Tmax | Time to reach Cmax | An indicator of absorption rate |
| t½ | Elimination half-life | Decline in concentration during the relevant elimination phase |

## 2. Absolute and relative bioavailability

**Absolute bioavailability** compares an extravascular route with an intravenous reference, accounting for dose.

<Formula label="Absolute bioavailability">F = (AUC oral × Dose IV) / (AUC IV × Dose oral)</Formula>

**Relative bioavailability** compares exposure from two non-IV products or formulations, with dose normalisation where needed.

## 3. What changes bioavailability?

- Drug solubility and dissolution rate.
- Permeability and intestinal transport.
- Gastric emptying and gastrointestinal transit.
- First-pass intestinal and hepatic metabolism.
- Food, formulation design and excipient effects.
- Chemical or enzymatic degradation in the gastrointestinal tract.

<Callout type="exam" title="First-pass effect">
A drug absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract may undergo metabolism in the intestinal wall and liver before reaching systemic circulation. This can reduce oral bioavailability.
</Callout>

## 4. Bioequivalence

Two pharmaceutical products are generally considered bioequivalent when their bioavailabilities are sufficiently similar under appropriately designed study conditions, such that there is no clinically meaningful difference in rate and extent of absorption.

For many conventional products, regulatory assessment commonly compares log-transformed AUC and Cmax. A frequently used acceptance interval for the 90% confidence interval of the test/reference geometric mean ratio is 80.00%–125.00%, subject to the applicable product-specific guidance and regulatory requirements.

<Callout type="caution" title="Do not treat 80–125% as a universal rule">
The acceptance criteria, study design and endpoints can vary for narrow therapeutic index drugs, highly variable drugs, modified-release products and other special cases. Always consult current regulatory guidance.
</Callout>

## GPAT quick revision

- AUC: extent of exposure.
- Cmax: peak concentration.
- Tmax: time to peak concentration; often used as an absorption-rate indicator.
- Absolute BA: extravascular vs IV reference, dose-normalised.
- IV administration has F = 1 (100%) by definition.
- First-pass metabolism can reduce oral bioavailability.
