## Start with the idea

A spectrophotometer sends light through a sample. The more light the sample absorbs, the less light passes through; under suitable conditions, absorbance helps estimate concentration.

UV–Visible spectroscopy measures how much ultraviolet or visible radiation a sample absorbs. Absorption occurs when molecules undergo electronic transitions.

## 1. Absorbance and transmittance

**Transmittance (T)** is the fraction of incident light that passes through the sample.

\[
T = \frac{I}{I_0}
\]

Where \(I_0\) is incident intensity and \(I\) is transmitted intensity.

**Percentage transmittance** is:

\[
\%T = 100 \times T
\]

**Absorbance (A)** is:

\[
A = \log_{10}\left(\frac{I_0}{I}\right) = -\log_{10}(T)
\]

<Callout type="exam" title="Relationship to remember">
Higher absorbance means lower transmittance. Absorbance is logarithmic, not a percentage.
</Callout>

## 2. Beer–Lambert equation

<Formula label="Beer–Lambert law">A = εbc</Formula>

- A = absorbance (dimensionless)
- ε = molar absorptivity / molar extinction coefficient
- b = optical path length (commonly cm)
- c = concentration (commonly mol L⁻¹ when ε is expressed in L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹)

At a fixed wavelength and path length, absorbance is directly proportional to concentration within the law's valid range.

## 3. Numerical example

A solution has transmittance 0.1.

\[
A = -\log_{10}(0.1) = 1
\]

If transmittance is 10%, convert it to 0.10 before taking the logarithm. Do not substitute 10 directly.

## 4. Calibration curve

A calibration curve plots absorbance against known concentrations. In the linear range, the slope is related to εb. The unknown concentration can be estimated from its measured absorbance using the validated calibration model.

<ProcessFlow title="Basic quantitative UV workflow">
<ol>
<li>Select a suitable analytical wavelength (often λmax after method development).</li>
<li>Prepare a blank and reference standard solutions.</li>
<li>Set the instrument and measure the blank.</li>
<li>Measure standards and construct the calibration curve.</li>
<li>Measure the sample under the same validated conditions.</li>
<li>Calculate concentration, applying dilution factors and method requirements.</li>
</ol>
</ProcessFlow>

## 5. Deviations from the law

Real systems may deviate from ideal linearity because of:
- High analyte concentration and molecular interactions.
- Chemical equilibria or changes in the absorbing species.
- Polychromatic radiation or excessive spectral bandwidth.
- Stray light and instrument limitations.
- Scattering, turbidity or an unsuitable blank.

## 6. Common GPAT traps

| Statement | Correct interpretation |
|---|---|
| Absorbance increases with concentration | True within the linear working range |
| Transmittance and absorbance increase together | False; they move in opposite directions |
| λmax is the wavelength of maximum absorbance | True |
| Absorbance has units of concentration | False; absorbance is dimensionless |
| Path length affects absorbance | True; A is proportional to b under ideal conditions |
