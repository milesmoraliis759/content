# UV–Visible Spectroscopy

UV–Visible spectroscopy measures the absorption of ultraviolet or visible radiation by a substance. In pharmaceutical analysis it is often used for quantitative estimation and method development.

## Principle

When a molecule absorbs radiation of a suitable wavelength, an electron can move from a lower-energy molecular orbital to a higher-energy molecular orbital.

Common electronic transitions discussed in UV spectroscopy include:
- σ → σ*
- n → σ*
- π → π*
- n → π*

The energy required depends on the molecule's electronic structure. Not every compound gives a useful UV–Visible absorption band; the molecule or a suitable derivative must absorb in the measured region.

## Chromophore and auxochrome

A **chromophore** is the part of a molecule associated with absorption in the UV–Visible region.

An **auxochrome** is a group that, when attached to a chromophore, can modify the wavelength or intensity of absorption.

Common descriptive terms:
- **Bathochromic shift:** movement of an absorption band to a longer wavelength.
- **Hypsochromic shift:** movement to a shorter wavelength.
- **Hyperchromic effect:** increase in absorption intensity.
- **Hypochromic effect:** decrease in absorption intensity.

These terms describe spectral changes; the actual cause depends on molecular structure and environment.

## Beer–Lambert law

For monochromatic light and suitable dilute solutions:

\[
A = \varepsilon b c
\]

Where:
- A = absorbance (dimensionless)
- ε = molar absorptivity
- b = path length (commonly cm)
- c = concentration

Absorbance is related to transmittance:

\[
A = -\log_{10}(T)
\]

where T is the fraction of incident light transmitted.

The law is useful for quantitative analysis when the method's conditions are controlled and the solution behaves within the applicable linear range.

## Main instrument components

1. **Radiation source:** provides suitable UV or visible radiation.
2. **Wavelength selector/monochromator:** selects a narrow wavelength band.
3. **Sample holder:** usually a cuvette of defined path length.
4. **Detector:** measures transmitted radiation.
5. **Readout/data system:** calculates and displays absorbance.

Quartz cells are used where UV transparency is required; ordinary glass absorbs strongly in parts of the UV region.

## Pharmaceutical applications

- Assay of suitable drug substances
- Dissolution sample analysis
- Estimation of compounds after colour development
- Reaction kinetics and stability studies
- Multicomponent analysis when the method is appropriately designed

## Common causes of deviation

High concentration, chemical association or dissociation, stray light, polychromatic radiation, turbidity, unsuitable solvent and instrumental limitations may cause deviation from ideal linearity.

## GPAT / NIPER revision

- Absorbance increases with concentration within the validated linear range.
- Path length is represented by b.
- Quartz cuvettes are generally preferred for UV measurements.
- A chromophore is associated with absorption; an auxochrome modifies its spectral behaviour.
- λmax is the wavelength of maximum absorbance under stated conditions.
