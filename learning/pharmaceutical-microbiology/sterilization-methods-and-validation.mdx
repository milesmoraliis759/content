# Sterilization

<Definition term="Sterilization">A validated process used to render a product free from viable microorganisms, including bacterial spores, to a specified sterility assurance level.</Definition>

Sterilization is not the same as disinfection. Disinfection reduces or eliminates many pathogenic microorganisms on inanimate objects but may not destroy bacterial spores.

## 1. Major methods

| Method | Principle | Common application examples |
|---|---|---|
| Moist heat | Protein denaturation / coagulation | Aqueous heat-stable materials, equipment and dressings |
| Dry heat | Oxidation and dehydration | Glassware, metal instruments, suitable oils and powders |
| Membrane filtration | Physical removal of microorganisms | Heat-sensitive solutions, when compatible and validated |
| Radiation | DNA damage | Selected disposable medical devices and materials |
| Ethylene oxide | Alkylation of cellular components | Heat- and moisture-sensitive compatible devices |
| Hydrogen peroxide vapour / plasma systems | Oxidative damage | Selected compatible instruments and devices |

## 2. Moist heat and autoclaving

Autoclaving uses saturated steam under pressure. Pressure allows steam to reach temperatures above the normal boiling point of water; moist heat transfers energy efficiently and destroys microorganisms.

A commonly taught reference cycle is **121 °C for about 15 minutes**, but this is not a universal cycle. Actual cycle parameters depend on load, packaging, equipment, product and validated heat penetration / lethality.

<Callout type="exam" title="Pressure is not the killing agent">
The principal sterilising agent is saturated steam at the required temperature and exposure conditions. Pressure enables the higher steam temperature.
</Callout>

## 3. Dry heat

Dry heat generally requires higher temperatures and longer exposure than moist heat because dry air transfers heat less efficiently than saturated steam.

It may be suitable for materials that tolerate high temperature and are damaged by moisture, such as certain glassware and oils.

## 4. Filtration

Membrane filtration removes microorganisms rather than killing them. A nominal 0.22 μm sterilising-grade filter is commonly used for many aqueous solutions, but filter suitability, adsorption, compatibility, integrity testing and aseptic handling must be validated.

## 5. Biological indicators

Biological indicators contain defined, viable microorganisms with known resistance characteristics for the sterilisation process being evaluated.

- Geobacillus stearothermophilus spores are commonly associated with moist-heat process monitoring.
- Bacillus atrophaeus spores are commonly associated with certain dry-heat and ethylene oxide processes.

The organism and indicator system must match the sterilisation method and applicable standard.

## 6. D-value

<Definition term="D-value">The decimal reduction time (D-value) is the time at a specified temperature and defined conditions required to reduce a microbial population by 90%, or one log₁₀ cycle.</Definition>

If the starting population is 10⁶ and six ideal decimal reductions occur, the expected surviving population is 10⁰ under the simplified model.

## GPAT quick revision

- Autoclave: moist heat / saturated steam.
- Dry heat: oxidation and dehydration.
- Filtration: removal, not killing.
- D-value: time for one log reduction.
- Biological indicators challenge the sterilisation process with resistant microorganisms.
- Sterility assurance requires validated processes and controlled handling.
