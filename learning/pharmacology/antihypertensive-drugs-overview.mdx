## Start with the idea

Blood pressure is influenced by how strongly the heart pumps and how tight the blood vessels are. Antihypertensive medicines lower pressure by acting on one or more of these controls.

Hypertension is persistently elevated arterial blood pressure. In simple terms, blood pressure depends mainly on how much blood the heart pumps and how much resistance the blood vessels offer.

\[
BP \approx CO \times TPR
\]

Here, CO is cardiac output and TPR is total peripheral resistance. Antihypertensive drugs lower one or both.

## 1. The major drug classes

| Class | Main mechanism | Representative drugs |
|---|---|---|
| ACE inhibitors | Reduce formation of angiotensin II; increase bradykinin | Enalapril, ramipril, lisinopril |
| ARBs | Block AT1 receptors | Losartan, valsartan, telmisartan |
| Calcium channel blockers | Reduce calcium entry, especially in vascular smooth muscle | Amlodipine, nifedipine; verapamil, diltiazem |
| Thiazide / thiazide-like diuretics | Increase renal sodium and water excretion | Hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone |
| Loop diuretics | Inhibit Na-K-2Cl cotransporter in thick ascending limb | Furosemide |
| Beta blockers | Reduce heart rate, contractility and renin release | Metoprolol, atenolol, bisoprolol |
| Alpha-1 blockers | Reduce vascular smooth-muscle tone | Prazosin, doxazosin |
| Direct vasodilators | Relax vascular smooth muscle | Hydralazine, minoxidil |

## 2. RAAS: the central pathway

<ProcessFlow title="Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system">
<ol>
<li>Reduced renal perfusion, reduced NaCl delivery or sympathetic stimulation promotes renin release.</li>
<li>Renin converts angiotensinogen into angiotensin I.</li>
<li>ACE converts angiotensin I into angiotensin II.</li>
<li>Angiotensin II causes vasoconstriction and stimulates aldosterone release.</li>
<li>Aldosterone promotes sodium and water retention, increasing blood volume.</li>
</ol>
</ProcessFlow>

<Callout type="exam" title="ACE inhibitor vs ARB">
ACE inhibitors reduce angiotensin II formation and inhibit bradykinin breakdown. ARBs block AT1 receptors but do not directly inhibit bradykinin breakdown. Therefore, cough and angioedema are more characteristic concerns with ACE inhibitors.
</Callout>

## 3. High-yield adverse effects

| Class | Important adverse effects / cautions |
|---|---|
| ACE inhibitors | Dry cough, hyperkalaemia, hypotension, angioedema; avoid in pregnancy |
| ARBs | Hyperkalaemia, hypotension; avoid in pregnancy |
| Dihydropyridine CCBs | Ankle oedema, flushing, headache |
| Verapamil | Bradycardia, constipation; caution with conduction disorders |
| Thiazides | Hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia, hyperuricaemia, increased glucose |
| Loop diuretics | Hypokalaemia, dehydration; ototoxicity risk at high exposure |
| Beta blockers | Bradycardia, fatigue; may mask adrenergic warning signs of hypoglycaemia |

## 4. Important comparisons

- **Dihydropyridines** such as amlodipine act predominantly on vascular smooth muscle.
- **Non-dihydropyridines** such as verapamil and diltiazem have more cardiac rate- and conduction-slowing effects.
- Thiazides act in the distal convoluted tubule; loop diuretics act in the thick ascending limb.
- Beta blockers are not interchangeable as a single clinical choice; indication, comorbidities and patient factors matter.

<Callout type="caution" title="Clinical context matters">
Drug selection depends on the patient's age, comorbidities, kidney function, pregnancy status, other medicines and current guidelines. This lesson is for examination learning, not self-treatment.
</Callout>

## GPAT quick revision

- ACE: decreases angiotensin II and increases bradykinin.
- ARB: blocks AT1 receptor.
- Amlodipine: dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker.
- Verapamil: non-dihydropyridine; constipation and bradycardia are classic associations.
- Thiazides: distal convoluted tubule.
- Furosemide: thick ascending limb; inhibits Na-K-2Cl transport.
